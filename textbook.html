Annotating a dense paragraph

There are 4 types of annotation we'll focus on: Question, Connection, Reflection, Investigate. On each screen, you'll see two example annotations for the same sentence—pick which one you think is the stronger example, then see why.

The passage we'll use

Read it once now. On each screen we'll highlight the part we're focusing on.

Russian psychologist Lev Vygotsky theorised that cognitive development advances within children's zone of proximal development (ZPD) (Vygotsky, 1978, 1997). The ZPD refers to the level of difficulty at which children can almost, but not quite, solve a problem independently. They are able to solve the problem if they are actively assisted by an adult or a more competent peer. By using scaffolding — the guidance, support and assistance that takes place in an interactive context — the less competent learner acquires new knowledge and skills. Scaffolding consists of hints, reminders, questions and prompts to advance the learner along the road of self-discovery. The teacher, sibling, peer or parent, however, must desist from doing too much for the learner, so that the learner does not merely become a passive onlooker.

Hoffnung (2022) 'Vygotsky' Lifespan Development, 5th Australasian Edition (5th ed). Wiley. Page 359.

Why this matters

Annotating isn't the end goal—it's the thinking that happens before you write. Every time you annotate, ask yourself:

Why am I making this annotation?

What will it help me do: build an argument, find evidence, question an assumption, connect ideas? Strong annotations are the first step towards strong paragraphs: each one is a small unit of thinking that can grow into a topic sentence, a piece of evidence, or a critical point in your writing.
